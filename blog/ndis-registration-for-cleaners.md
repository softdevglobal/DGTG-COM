# NDIS Registration for Cleaners: Do Cleaning Businesses Need to Register?

**Target keyword:** NDIS registration for cleaners  
**Last reviewed:** 30 August 2026

**NDIS registration for cleaners** is not mandatory in every situation. A cleaning business must be registered to deliver supports funded through an NDIA-managed plan. A self-managed or plan-managed participant can usually use either a registered or unregistered cleaner, unless the service falls within a category for which registration is specifically mandatory.

For a cleaner who wants access to NDIA-managed participants, stronger referral credibility and a listing in the registered-provider market, registration may still be commercially valuable even where it is not compulsory.

## Which registration group usually applies to NDIS cleaning?

The registration group most commonly associated with domestic cleaning is:

> **0120 — Household tasks**

The NDIS Quality and Safeguards Commission lists Household Tasks as a **verification** registration group. Verification is the lower-risk audit pathway and normally involves an approved quality auditor completing a desktop review of the required documentary evidence.

Do not assume that every service a cleaning company offers fits within 0120. The correct scope depends on what the business actually delivers.

Examples that may require separate consideration include:

- gardening or yard maintenance;
- minor home repairs;
- transport;
- personal care;
- work in a supported independent living arrangement;
- specialised cleaning involving clinical waste, infection risks or hazardous substances;
- services bundled with another higher-risk support.

If the application includes any certification registration group, the Commission requires a certification audit rather than a verification-only audit.

## When must an NDIS cleaner be registered?

Registration is required where the cleaner wants to deliver an NDIS support funded through an **NDIA-managed** plan. The NDIS states that NDIA-managed participants must use registered providers.

For other ordinary cleaning supports, participants with self-managed or plan-managed funding can usually choose registered or unregistered providers.

The practical decision can be shown as a simple flow:

```text
Will you serve NDIA-managed participants?
        |
       Yes  → Registration required
        |
        No
        |
Will you deliver a support that is otherwise subject to mandatory registration?
        |
       Yes  → Registration required
        |
        No
        |
Registration may be optional, but can still support market access and trust.
```

A cleaner should not advertise itself as a registered NDIS provider until the Commission has issued the registration.

## What does a verification audit look at?

The Commission explains that a verification audit is a desktop review conducted by an Approved Quality Auditor. The exact evidence depends on the provider, business structure and registration scope.

A cleaning business should be prepared to organise evidence in these areas.

### Business identity and insurance

- Australian Business Number and legal entity details;
- business name details;
- public liability insurance;
- workers compensation where staff are employed;
- other insurance appropriate to the service;
- consistent legal names across the application, policies and insurance certificates.

### Worker suitability

- NDIS Worker Screening clearances where workers perform risk-assessed roles for a registered provider;
- identity and right-to-work records;
- role descriptions;
- induction records;
- training records;
- contractor compliance records where subcontractors are used.

### Safe service delivery

- risk assessments;
- chemical handling and safety data sheets;
- personal protective equipment procedures;
- infection prevention practices;
- manual handling procedures;
- lone-worker and home-entry procedures;
- keys, alarms and property-access controls;
- equipment inspection and maintenance records.

### Participant rights and communication

- service agreements;
- privacy and confidentiality;
- consent and communication preferences;
- complaint information;
- incident reporting;
- participant-property damage process;
- cancellation and pricing terms;
- procedures for identifying and escalating a safeguarding concern.

### Operational records

The auditor is interested in whether the system can work in practice. Useful records include:

- a worker compliance register;
- incident and complaint registers;
- equipment and chemical registers;
- training records;
- service checklists;
- job completion records;
- continuous-improvement records.

## A cleaning policy pack must match the real business

A sole trader who cleans participants’ homes needs a different system from a company employing 40 cleaners across multiple states.

Documents should accurately state:

- who receives complaints;
- who reviews incidents;
- how workers are screened;
- how chemicals are transported and stored;
- how access keys are controlled;
- how a missed service is managed;
- what happens if property is damaged;
- how participant information is protected;
- how an emergency or unsafe home environment is handled.

Do not approve a policy claiming that the company has a compliance committee, clinical governance team or 24-hour manager if none of those things exists.

## What does registration cost?

The Commission says there is no cost to register with it, but the provider must pay an Approved Quality Auditor. The Commission does not set audit prices and recommends obtaining multiple quotes.

The total setup budget may also include:

- registration preparation;
- personalised documents;
- insurance;
- worker screening;
- training;
- equipment and PPE;
- record-keeping systems;
- corrective-action work if the auditor identifies gaps.

NDIS Provider Registration charges a **$500 registration-support service fee**. The approved auditor is independent and charges separately.

## Should a small cleaner register?

Registration may make sense when the business wants to:

- serve NDIA-managed participants;
- appear in the registered-provider market;
- build referral relationships with organisations that prefer registered providers;
- create consistent policies and worker controls before hiring staff;
- position itself for broader disability-service contracts.

It may be less urgent when the cleaner serves only self-managed or plan-managed participants and does not intend to expand. The business should weigh the market opportunity against the audit and ongoing compliance workload.

## Common mistakes cleaners make

1. Selecting personal-care registration groups when the business only provides domestic cleaning.
2. Adding gardening, maintenance or transport without scoping those services separately.
3. Treating an ordinary commercial cleaning manual as a complete NDIS evidence system.
4. Failing to screen subcontractors.
5. Holding insurance under a different entity from the applicant.
6. Having no participant complaint or incident pathway.
7. Using generic policies that do not match the workforce.
8. Assuming registration ends after the initial audit.

## The next step

Write a precise list of every service the cleaning business will provide. Separate cleaning, gardening, maintenance, transport and personal support instead of combining them under one broad description. That list determines the registration groups and audit scope.
